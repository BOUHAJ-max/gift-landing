Gifts from the Deep

"Every genuine inner experience leaves a residue. Not the memory of the experience — memory fades, distorts, reconstructs. The residue is something different: a calibration, a reorientation, a permanent adjustment in the quality of attention brought to everything that follows."

He is sometimes asked — by people who have read about his practice, or heard him speak, or simply encountered his writing and felt in it the signature of someone who has been somewhere unusual — what he got from it. What the out-of-body travel gave him that he did not have before. What the five years of practice produced that justified the risk and the disorientation and the eventual decision to close the door.

It is a good question, and he takes it seriously, because the honest answer is not the simple one. The simple answer would be: extraordinary experiences. The expanded sense of what is possible. The verification of several key claims of the mystical tradition through direct experience rather than faith alone. These are real. They are not the deepest part of the answer.

The deepest part of the answer is about quality of attention. About what happens to ordinary consciousness when extraordinary consciousness has been experienced and the ordinary world has been returned to. About the permanent recalibration — small, quiet, largely invisible to anyone watching from outside — that occurs when a person has been to places that ordinary consciousness does not access and has come back knowing that those places are real.

"The traveler who returns from the extraordinary and pretends nothing has changed is lying. The traveler who returns from the extraordinary and can speak of nothing else has not yet integrated the journey. The traveler who returns and finds the ordinary world subtly transformed — more present, more itself, more worth the full quality of attention — has understood what the journey was for."

The first gift is this: the permanent diminishment of the tyranny of the ordinary. By which he means — not the dismissal of the ordinary, which would be a mistake, but the release from the assumption that the ordinary is all there is. This sounds small. It is not small. Most of the suffering that human beings experience that is not caused by straightforward physical pain or material deprivation is caused by the imprisonment in the ordinary — the inability to conceive of any register of experience beyond what the ordinary categories provide. Depression is often, at its root, the experience of being trapped in a version of reality that is too small for the person experiencing it. The mystical traditions have always known this. The inner world — whatever its ultimate nature — is an exit from that prison. Not the only exit, and not a complete solution, but a genuine door.

The second gift is what he calls the evidence-base for faith. He was raised in a tradition that asks for faith — genuine, active, orientational faith in what cannot be directly verified by ordinary sensory experience. He was not raised in a tradition that encourages the testing of faith through direct experience, partly because direct experience of the non-ordinary was understood, in the popular version of the tradition, to be either miraculous (and therefore not available on demand) or dangerous (and therefore to be avoided by those without proper guidance). The five years of practice gave him direct experience of the non-ordinary. Some of it was consistent with what the tradition predicted. Some of it was more ambiguous. All of it shifted the epistemological basis of his faith from the purely doctrinal to the partly experiential.

This is not a claim that his faith is now verified in the scientific sense. It is a claim that his faith now rests on a different kind of ground — a ground that includes direct encounter with the territory the tradition maps, however partial and imperfect and uncertain that encounter was. He finds this ground more stable than pure doctrine, not because it is more certain but because it is more honest. He has not taken on faith something he has never tested. He has tested what he could test, found the testing consistent with the tradition's basic claims, and extended his faith into the territory beyond what testing can reach on the basis of that consistency. This seems to him the most rational available relationship between faith and evidence.

The Third Gift: The Language of the Tradition

He was always interested in the Sufi tradition, always drawn to its literature and its claims. But interest is not understanding, and before the years of practice, his understanding of the tradition's descriptions of inner experience was primarily literary — he understood what the words meant, could parse the Arabic, could follow the argument, but had no experiential referent for the states the tradition described.

After the practice, this changed. When Ibn Arabi writes about the imaginal world — the alam al-khayal, the intermediate realm where spirit takes form and form dissolves into spirit — Marudai now has a referent. Not a complete referent, not a claim to have fully experienced what Ibn Arabi describes, but a partial one: he has been in territory that resembles what is being described, and the description now means something different than it meant before. The words are no longer exclusively literary. They are, partially, technical: descriptions of states that have phenomenological content he can partially match.

This changes the way he reads the tradition. Not making it simpler — if anything, it becomes more complex, because he can now see the places where his experience does not match the description, which raises questions that pure literary understanding does not generate. But it makes it more alive. The tradition is not a collection of beautiful ideas about an invisible world. It is a map of territory that is actually there. The map has limitations and areas of uncertainty and places where different mapmakers disagree. But it is a map of something real. He knows this now, not from faith alone, but from the combination of faith and the particular kind of dirty, uncertain, unverifiable-but-genuine evidence that direct experience provides.

"The greatest gift of the inner journey is not what you find there. It is what you bring back. The what you find there is extraordinary and beautiful and sometimes terrifying and always, in the end, insufficient without the what you bring back. The what you bring back is the only thing that can change the quality of the ordinary. And the quality of the ordinary is the quality of a life."

The fourth gift — and he says this knowing it sounds like the kind of thing that is easy to say and hard to mean — is gratitude. Not the performed gratitude of someone who has been taught that gratitude is a spiritual virtue and is therefore practicing it. The specific, unasked-for, sometimes inconvenient gratitude that arises when you have seen the universe from the outside — or whatever the astral travel was — and come back to a cup of tea and a window and the sound of birds in the early morning.

The universe is enormous. The consciousness that can perceive it is, in the scale of the universe, vanishingly small. And that vanishingly small consciousness, in the form of a man sitting at a window in Casablanca with a cup of tea, is somehow capable of experiencing the enormity — of having some relationship, however partial and mediated and uncertain, with what is actually there. This is extraordinary. It should produce gratitude. It does produce gratitude, when the memory of the enormity is sufficiently present in the ordinary moment to provide the contrast that makes the gift visible.

He cultivates this memory. Not obsessively. Not as a spiritual practice in the formal sense. Simply as attention — the occasional deliberate return to what he knows about the scale of what exists, and the contrast with the specific, particular, irreplaceable smallness of this moment, this cup, this window, this morning. The contrast does not diminish the moment. It illuminates it. It makes the ordinary strange in the way that the ordinary, honestly attended to, always is.

What Remains

What remains, after everything — after the extraordinary journeys and the conversations with impossible sages and the future ruins and the galactic travel and the failed confrontation with the devil and the night the self dissolved and all the rest — is the ordinary. The five daily prayers. The reading. The writing. The conversations with people who do not know and do not need to know any of what is in this book. The cup of tea at the window. The morning light.

And in all of it, persistent and quiet and entirely reliable, the quality he has no adequate word for except presence. The quality of being fully in what is actually happening, without the usual degree of distraction into what might be or should be or was or could be. The quality that the contemplative traditions have always pointed toward as the goal — not the extraordinary experiences, not the visionary states, not the out-of-body travel or the inner illuminations, but this: the simple, complete, entirely ordinary and entirely extraordinary fact of being fully here.

He arrived at this not by seeking it directly but by going very far in the other direction and coming back. By being so thoroughly outside the ordinary — so completely in the extraordinary — that the ordinary, on return, was restored to its proper strangeness. Its proper weight. Its proper claim on his full attention.

He does not recommend his specific route to anyone. The route is not important. The destination — this quality of presence, this restoration of the ordinary to its full reality — is what matters. And the destination is available from many starting points, through many paths, at many different paces. The only requirement is the refusal to settle for less than what is actually available.

Which is, in the end, the only requirement for a rebel.

Stand tall, O Dragon. You are not one of those who retreat to catch their breath. Your every breath is fire without fuel. And when you release it, it illuminates — not destroys — everything it touches.

This is the fire that was always meant.
