The Science of What Cannot Be Measured

"Modern science is the most powerful tool humanity has ever built for understanding one register of reality. It is completely useless for several others. Knowing which register you are working in is the beginning of genuine intelligence."

The field of parapsychology — the academic study of phenomena that fall outside the current explanatory framework of mainstream science — occupies an uncomfortable position in the intellectual landscape: too rigorous to be dismissed as mere mysticism, too anomalous to be comfortably integrated into the conventional scientific worldview. It persists, funded marginally, staffed by researchers who accept a significant professional cost for their choice of subject matter, producing results that are statistically significant enough to require explanation but not repeatable on demand in controlled conditions in the way that conventional science requires for its most confident claims.

Marudai has followed this literature for years. He reads the major figures — Dean Radin, whose work on the statistics of psi phenomena is careful and extensive; Pim van Lommel, whose prospective study of near-death experiences in cardiac patients is perhaps the most methodologically rigorous investigation of the survival-of-consciousness question ever conducted; the researchers at the Monroe Institute whose documentation of out-of-body experiences has produced a large body of verified cases. He takes the work seriously as data.

"The scientist who dismisses consciousness as mere epiphenomenon has not explained what the epiphenomenon is made of. The mystic who dismisses science as irrelevant has not explained how his spiritual truth handles the problem of evidence. Both are running away from the same hard question."

At the risk of being more explicit than is comfortable, he will state here what he actually believes, as clearly as he can state it. He believes that consciousness is not produced by the brain the way a factory produces a product. He believes the brain is more like a receiver — extraordinarily sophisticated, capable of modulation and selective tuning, but ultimately a receiver for a signal that exists independent of the hardware. He believes this because he has experienced states in which the hardware was functionally inactive — the body in deep paralysis, the ordinary cognitive processes suspended — while the signal was stronger and clearer than in ordinary waking operation.

He believes the human being has a second body — an etheric vehicle, a subtle form — capable of functioning independently of the physical body under certain conditions. He believes the flood verification, among other experiences, requires some such explanation if it is to be explained at all. He believes there are forces in the invisible world that are neither benevolent nor neutral — forces that can be attracted by specific practices and that should be treated with serious caution. And he believes the Islamic tradition, as articulated in the Dar al-A'maq framework, provides the most coherent and practically useful map of the inner territory that he has encountered — more useful than the Western parapsychological framework, more useful than any spiritual marketplace that has neither data nor cosmology.

He holds all of these beliefs with the humility appropriate to a person still in the middle of the exploration — not at its end, not at a vantage point from which the whole territory is visible, but at the point on the path that the path has brought him to today. Tomorrow the path may bring him to a different point. He will report from there when he arrives.
