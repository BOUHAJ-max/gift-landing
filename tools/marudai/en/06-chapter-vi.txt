A Flood Fifty Kilometers Away

"The second body does not lie. It goes where it goes. It sees what it sees. What you do with the verification is your responsibility."

Of all the projections in the five-year record, this one has the most straightforward claim to objective validity. He went somewhere real. He witnessed something real. He confirmed it through independent means one hour later.

It was a Sunday morning in 2019. He was in the first year of his high school baccalaureate. The projection was triggered by the standard transitional phase approximately nine in the morning — a time he had found consistently productive for the practice, when the body had rested sufficiently from the night but had not yet been fully claimed by the waking day.

The separation was clean and immediate. He had been practicing regularly enough by this point that the mechanics of exit had become, if not effortless, then at least familiar — the way a mountaineer becomes familiar with a difficult climb, not by making it easy but by understanding its specific demands well enough to meet them without wasting energy on surprise.

He decided to visit a village he knew well, located approximately fifty kilometers from his position. He knew its geography: the wide dry riverbed (wadi) that ran through its center, the stands of date palms on its banks, the way the surrounding hills converged above it to create a catchment area that could, in heavy rain years, produce serious flooding.

"I went where I had not planned to go, saw what I could not have imagined, and came back to find it was true. This is not proof of anything supernatural. It is proof that the categories we use to define the natural are too small."

Arrival was instantaneous. He found himself suspended in the air above the wadi, at approximately the height of the taller palm trees — close enough to see individual fronds moving in the morning breeze, far enough to take in the shape of the valley as a whole. The atmosphere was overcast — a grey-white sky with diffuse light, the kind of day that muffles sound and flattens shadows. The wadi below him was dry, as it usually was in this season.

He hovered, observing. The detail was precise: he could identify individual trees by their shapes, see the paths that ran along the wadi's banks, make out the architectural features of the nearest houses — the rooftop water tank of one house, the unpainted cement of a newer construction, the old carved wooden door of a house whose family had lived in it for four generations.

Then, in the distance up the wadi — in the direction of the hills from which any seasonal flow would originate — he saw it. A line of turbid brown water, moving through the dry riverbed with the particular rolling, churning motion that distinguishes a flash flood from ordinary river flow. The front of the flood was perhaps a meter or so in height, carrying the familiar cargo of branches, plastic waste, and the dark organic matter of disturbed riverbeds.

He watched it approach for what felt like two or three minutes. The sound was the low rumble of moving water and rolling stones — a sound that anyone who has grown up in the mountainous regions of Morocco will immediately recognize as the voice of a wadi coming to life. Then, as the water reached the area below him and began to spread across the wadi floor, he felt the familiar pull of the physical body and returned.

An hour later, he called family members in that village. They confirmed: the flood had come that morning. It had arrived exactly in the period he had been observing it, from exactly the direction he had seen it approach, with the scale and character he had witnessed.

He had not known the flood was coming. The weather in his own location gave no indication. He had no information about conditions in the hills above that wadi. The verification was clean, the confirmation unambiguous, the experience — whatever its ultimate metaphysical status — repeatable in principle by anyone willing to cultivate the same capacity.
