The Mad Sage at the Gate of Three Trenches

"A man who has never been genuinely bewildered by a fool has not yet met a fool. The deepest wisdom sometimes wears the most absurd clothing."

He found the sage at the foot of the wall near Bab al-Khandaq al-Thalatha — the Gate of the Three Trenches — in the early decades of the seventeenth century, sitting as still as a Pharaonic statue carved from pure disillusionment. His hands were turning small stones over and over in his lap, with the focused attention of a man solving a problem that has no solution and knows it.

Marudai had arrived there without planning to arrive there, which was becoming a pattern. The second body, in its more advanced stages of use, had its own ideas about destination. He had intended to explore the medina of Marrakech in one of its historical layers, and the second body had taken him here instead — to this specific wall, in this specific decade, to this specific man.

He sat beside the sage without announcing himself. The sage's wooden prayer beads scattered across the ground when Marudai's presence disturbed the air — or whatever it was that the second body disturbed in the physical world when it came close enough to interact with it. The sage looked at the scattered beads without apparent concern, then looked at Marudai with an expression that combined severe intelligence with the absolute calm of someone who has been surprised by exactly nothing for a very long time.

"I know who you are. You are the commander whose arrival was announced to us. Tell me what you want and stop pretending you are simply passing through."

Marudai asked him what he was doing at this hour, in this cold, turning stones. The sage spent a long time gathering his prayer beads before answering, picking each one up individually with a deliberateness that managed to be both tedious and somehow fascinating.

"The rules and the absurd serve the same void. Remove one and the other becomes meaningless. This is why the scholar and the madman are always sitting at the same table, arguing about who ordered what."

The sage had been studying the weasel, he said. He had been attempting to extract its symbolic significance by examining it against the loom of opposites and equivalences that constituted, in his philosophical framework, the underlying structure of reality. He had found the weasel's significance to be exactly zero. This had led him, by a chain of reasoning he described with great seriousness, to compare it with the frightened rabbit, which he found to occupy an equivalent position in the scheme of meaninglessness. From there he had extrapolated to the chicken — a creature of remarkable equanimity — and the jackal, who wore the mask of violence but was, at his core, a creature of profound domestic instinct. The juxtaposition had allowed him to see, briefly, with unusual clarity. He had then lost the clarity. He was trying to find it again by returning to the beginning.

Marudai told him about a problem he had been having in his own time: a professor who had become angry because Marudai had written 'translate to Arabic' using one Arabic preposition rather than another — a distinction so minute that most fluent speakers of the language would not register it, but one that the professor felt represented a fundamental error of intention.

The sage laughed — a laugh that did not reach his eyes, which remained watchful and serious through the performance of amusement.

"You are both correct within your domains. You are both wrong to make your domain absolute. The professor wants the end-point of the action. You want the ultimate destination of the purpose. The rules and the absurd serve the same void. Remove either line from the ledger and both become senseless."

He told the sage about the strangest day of his life: a pain that had felled him, sudden and total, that a small child had cured by instructing him to strike a donkey with a stick cut from a dead oak tree. He had done it. He had been cured immediately, without residue or explanation.

The sage looked at him for a long time with an expression Marudai could not read, and then smiled — very small, very private, the smile of someone acknowledging a point they had been expecting to hear for some time.

"There is no meaning in meaning, my son. The action that breaks logic is the only truth that will save you from the illusion of organization. Continue on your path."

He struck his walking stick once against the ground. And Marudai found himself back in his own time, in his own body, flooded with an energy that had no name in any of the languages he spoke.
