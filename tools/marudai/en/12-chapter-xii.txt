The Imaginal World and the Real World

"There is a world that reflects you back to yourself. And there is a world that shows you what is actually there. Most practitioners never learn to tell the difference. This is the great tragedy of mystical exploration."

After years of practice and hundreds of individual experiences, the question that occupied Marudai most was not whether these experiences had happened — they clearly had, in some sense, and the flood verification had given him at least one clean data point that resisted purely internal explanations. The question that occupied him was more fundamental: what were they experiences of?

The Islamic esoteric tradition, specifically as articulated in the Dar al-A'maq framework, offered a distinction that he found more useful than anything the Western parapsychological literature provided: the distinction between the imaginal inner world (al-batin al-khayali) and the real inner world (al-batin al-haqiqi).

The imaginal inner world is real. Let this be stated clearly, because the word imaginal tends, in the contemporary ear, to activate associations with the fictional, the invented, the not-quite-there. The imaginal inner world is a genuine layer of reality, with its own laws, its own geography, its own population of forms and forces. But it has a characteristic that distinguishes it from higher registers of inner reality: it is responsive to the consciousness that enters it. What you find in the imaginal world reflects, in profound and sometimes barely recognizable ways, what you carry into it.

"The mirror does not lie. But it can only show you yourself. If you want to see what is actually there, you need a window, not a mirror. Most people searching for the truth are looking into mirrors and calling it revelation."

This is why practitioners from different religious traditions, entering the same imaginal world, encounter such different things. The Christian practitioner encounters a version of heaven consistent with Christian iconography. The Buddhist practitioner encounters pure lands and bodhisattvas. The Islamic practitioner encounters the forms that Islamic tradition has populated the invisible world with. All of them are having genuine experiences. All of them are, to some extent, generating the content of those experiences from their own prior beliefs and imaginative frameworks.

This does not make the experiences meaningless. The imaginal world is not merely a cinema of the self. It has genuine reality — the ocean does not cease to be an ocean because the swimmer brings their own body chemistry to it. But it means that the cosmological claims extracted from imaginal world exploration must be treated with considerable caution. The tradition that tells you the concentric rings you encountered on the boundary of the observable universe are the heavens — and that the world with the red hills was beyond the seventh heaven — may be right. Or it may be that your own prior knowledge of traditional cosmology generated those images, and the tradition's account is being confirmed by the tradition's own projection. The epistemological challenge is genuine and cannot be dissolved by enthusiasm.

The real inner world operates by a completely different logic. It does not confirm your beliefs. It often contradicts them. Information is transmitted that the practitioner could not have generated from their own knowledge base. The Quran is heard there as it is written — not as the practitioner remembers it, with the errors and gaps of human memory, but exactly. Authentic figures from the tradition appear without being summoned and without serving the practitioner's preferences. And the states produced have a quality and intensity that goes beyond what the imaginal world generates — not louder, not more dramatic, but more real, in the specific sense of carrying information that could not have been produced internally.

Marudai does not claim to have reliably reached the real inner world through astral projection. He claims something more modest and more honest: he is not certain that he did. Some of what he experienced had the quality-markers of the imaginal. Some of it — particularly the flood — had characteristics that the imaginal world alone does not easily explain. The honest position is to hold both possibilities simultaneously, without forcing a resolution that the evidence does not support.
