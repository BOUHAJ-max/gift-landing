The Night He Called for the Devil

"The bravest thing is not the absence of fear. It is the decision to act in the presence of fear because the action is necessary. The second bravest thing is knowing when the action is not necessary."

He came home late from a long journey, exhausted in the specific way that long travel produces — the body overused, the mind still processing miles of road, the night already deep by the time he lay down. He did not intend to project. He had no energy for intention. He simply lay down and let whatever came, come.

What came was involuntary exit — the body releasing consciousness into the second form without any deliberate contribution from him, the way a river releases water over a fall that was always there and always going to be reached. He found himself in a space he did not recognize: an architectural complex, old buildings in various states of preservation and disrepair. The light was the moonlit quality he had come to associate with certain registers of the inner world — low, silver, shadowless, everything visible but nothing highlighted.

He was, in this state, filled with a quality he must describe honestly even if the description sounds like the grandiosity he has just spent a chapter warning against: absolute confidence. Not ordinary confidence, not the familiar mixture of self-belief and residual self-doubt that characterizes most human action. Something more foundational — the specific absence of the inner negotiation between capability and limitation that normally accompanies every moment of active engagement. Pure will, with no resistance.

He engaged — with what or whom he cannot be entirely certain — and he dominated. The encounter was brief, decisive, characterized by the specific quality of force that is not muscular effort but directional intent, a willing of displacement that the second body executes without the mechanical mediation that physical action requires. The figure, whatever it was, was gone. And then, in the state of absolute confidence he was still inhabiting, he had the idea. He would call for Iblis. The Shaytan. He would settle the matter.

"Absolute confidence without absolute wisdom is the most dangerous state available to a human being. It has produced most of the disasters in history. It has also produced most of the achievements. The difference lies entirely in the direction it is pointed."

"If you have the courage, show yourself. Come and fight. I am here."

He waited. He scanned the spaces between the buildings. He climbed to the top of a ruined wall and stood there, fully visible, a statement of availability in every direction. Nothing came. He waited longer. The moonlit silence continued. He was, in the moment, disappointed. The absolute confidence of the state included a genuine readiness for a confrontation he was certain he would win, because the state left no room for any other possibility.

He tells this story now with the amusement and slight embarrassment that belong to accounts of one's younger stupidities. The tradition is explicit: you do not go looking for the forces of darkness. You do not invite confrontation with them. You do not treat the inner world as an arena for the display of power, however real that power might be. The forces of darkness are not impressed by power. They are expert at it. They have been working with it for longer than any human being has been working with anything. The appropriate response is not greater force but correct orientation — the specific quality of being genuinely rooted in the Real that makes the forces of darkness fundamentally irrelevant, not because they have been defeated but because the ground they operate on is not the ground you are standing on. The night of the devil's absence is one of his clearest data points.
