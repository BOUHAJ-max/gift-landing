The Book of Movements — Why the Body is an Antenna

"Every position the body takes is a prayer addressed to a specific force. The question is not whether you are praying. The question is to what."

For years, Marudai was haunted by a correlation he could not explain. It appeared with uncomfortable regularity: whenever he fell asleep on his back, particularly after evenings of intense spiritual practice — the recitation of divine names, extended contemplation, the careful reading of Islamic esoteric texts — something happened that would not happen if he slept on his side.

The experiences varied. Sometimes he found himself in a state of paralysis and buzzing awareness, conscious of his room but unable to move, hearing sounds like electrical engines spinning inside his skull. Sometimes he simply appeared, without transition, in a foreign landscape — mountains, a beach, a room he recognized from waking life but was not physically in. And sometimes, much later in his practice, he embarked on journeys that can only be described as voyages to the edges of the known universe.

The connection to the sleeping position nagged at him. It seemed absurd on its face. How could something as trivial as the direction his spine was pointing determine whether or not he would, in the next hour, be standing on a beach in an island he had never visited, watching turquoise water and feeling — not imagining, feeling — the sand between his toes?

"The Prophet, peace be upon him, did not prescribe the right side as a sleeping position because it was comfortable. He prescribed it because he knew what the left side collects — and what the right side refuses."

He found his answer in a series of books published by two Moroccan scholars — Dr. Tihami Mohammed El-Wakili and Professor Nour-Eddine Abdelhaq — under the imprint of Dar al-A'maq, a publishing house whose name translates as The House of the Depths. The series, produced in Morocco in the 1980s, addressed what the authors called the esoteric sciences of religion — the inner mechanisms behind the external forms of Islamic practice.

The key book was titled Sirr al-Harakat: The Secret of Movements. Its thesis was simple in statement and profound in implication: every physical position the human body adopts attracts a specific category of energy. The authors classified these energies as forces of light (noor) or forces of darkness (dhulumat) — not as metaphors, but as literal descriptions of what the subtle body encounters when placed in particular orientations relative to the invisible world.

The sleeping positions recommended in the Prophetic tradition, seen through this lens, were not arbitrary hygiene rules or cultural customs. They were precise energetic prescriptions. Sleeping on the right side attracts forces of light — energies that support mental clarity, spiritual receptivity, and what the authors call the nourishing function of sleep. Sleeping on the left attracts forces of darkness — not necessarily evil in a simple moral sense, but disorganizing, incoherent energies that fragment consciousness and produce the strange phenomena Marudai had been experiencing without understanding.

The Experiment He Did Not Want to Do

Skeptical by nature — the kind of skepticism that comes not from closed-mindedness but from having been surprised enough times to know that reality is more complex than any single theory can contain — Marudai decided to test the left-side hypothesis directly. He consciously adopted the left-side position after a period of focused concentration and waited, with full attention, for whatever would come.

What came was unmistakable. Within minutes, he felt a pressure building in a specific region — the front-left quadrant of his skull. It was unlike any ordinary headache. It was accompanied by a sensation he can only describe as static electricity coursing through the tissues of his brain, concentrated in that forward-left region, as though a radio receiver had been tuned to a frequency that the system was not designed to handle.

The images that followed, as he approached sleep, were bizarre in a very specific way. He saw a scorpion's tail with a mouth that opened and closed. He saw Mandala-like patterns that were disturbing rather than beautiful — asymmetric, jarring, as though someone had taken a sacred geometric form and introduced a subtle error into its construction that made the eye keep seeking a symmetry that was not there. He saw sequences that had no logical structure whatsoever.

Most telling was the aftermath. When he woke, the sensation did not immediately dissipate. It lingered, producing a subtle disorientation — mood fluctuations, a vague headache, a feeling of mental cloudiness that he had no other word for than interference. It was only when he deliberately spent time lying on his right side that the sensation gradually cleared, like a radio signal steadying after tuning.

"The body knows what the mind has not yet been told. Listen to your body with the same attention you give to your scripture. It is scripture written in flesh."

This experiment, along with many others over the following years, convinced Marudai of something that shaped everything else he came to understand about consciousness exploration: the religious traditions that contain sleeping and movement instructions are not culturally arbitrary. They are the accumulated wisdom of people who explored these territories long before him, who mapped the dangers, and who encoded their findings in the most durable storage medium available — religious obligation.

Prophet Seth — Shith, peace be upon him — is described in the esoteric tradition as having been sent to the region that would later be called China, with a specific mission: to teach the correct science of movements. The physical disciplines that emerged from that region were, in their original form, expressions of a divinely revealed system of correct bodily orientation. What humanity now practices as yoga, tai chi, and similar systems are, according to this tradition, later and modified versions — some beneficial, many distorted to the point of attracting the very forces they once protected against.

He presented this not as established scientific fact but as a framework — one that became, for him, essential for understanding what he was experiencing. Without it, his journeys out of the body were simply fascinating. With it, they became data points in a much larger map.
