What the Abdal Know

"The Abdal are not saints in the comfortable sense. They are travelers between worlds who have learned to bring back something useful. This is much harder than it sounds."

The tradition of the Abdal — the Substitutes, the forty spiritual poles who maintain the world's equilibrium in the Islamic cosmological framework — has always fascinated Marudai not for its supernatural claims but for what it implies about the relationship between consciousness and embodiment.

The Abdal are called Substitutes because they can substitute themselves. Their second body — the subtle, etheric vehicle that everyone possesses but few ever learn to use — has been developed to such a degree of mastery that it can operate independently of the physical body, travel to distant locations, appear in the physical world as a genuine material presence, and perform functions that the physical body cannot perform without being physically present.

A single Abdal, the tradition claims, can multiply themselves forty times over — forty simultaneous deployments of the second body, each one appearing in a different location, each one fully operative, each one capable of speech, physical contact, and the transmission of the specific baraka (spiritual blessing) that the Abdal carries. Meanwhile the physical body remains in its place, breathing normally, maintaining its physical life as usual.

"The Abdal are not special because God made them special. They are special because they took the teaching seriously enough to actually do the work. The gift was available to everyone. Most people chose comfort instead."

Marudai notes this tradition not to claim membership in it — he is explicit that he makes no such claim — but because it indicates something important about the Islamic tradition's understanding of what the second body actually is and what it is actually for. This is not a folk tradition, not a marginal curiosity. The Abdal are discussed in authenticated Hadith literature, in the major works of Islamic spirituality, in the treatises of scholars whose credentials the orthodox tradition accepts without hesitation. The subtle body and its capacities are not inventions of New Age speculation. They are a recognized feature of the Islamic understanding of the human being.

What distinguishes the Abdal's second-body mastery from the kind of projection that Marudai practiced for five years is precisely what distinguishes the real inner world from the imaginal: the Abdal's capacities emerge from genuine spiritual development — from decades or lifetimes of authentic religious practice, inner purification, and the gradual emergence of capacities that are, in the tradition's framework, natural to the human being at higher stages of development. Not supernatural. Natural at the level of the complete human being — al-insan al-kamil — which is the level the tradition is pointing toward when it talks about what we could be if we fully inhabited what we are.

Astral projection as a technique — divorced from genuine religious development, practiced as an end in itself or as a form of exploration for its own sake — produces experiences that resemble the Abdal's capacities the way a child's drawing resembles a master's painting. The formal similarity is real. The depth of the thing — the rootedness in something genuine — is categorically different.

This was the conclusion Marudai arrived at, slowly and with considerable resistance, over the course of his five years of practice. The practice had given him experiences of extraordinary value. And it had also shown him its own limits — the limits of any technology that is not grounded in the deeper work of becoming.
