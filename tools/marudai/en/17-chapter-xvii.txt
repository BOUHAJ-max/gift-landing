Letters to the Impossible

"I write to things that cannot read. I speak to silences that have no ears. And somehow, something always answers. This is the evidence I have for what I believe."

Among the documents Marudai keeps — not in any physical archive, but in the specific memory that belongs to experiences of sufficient intensity to be permanently installed — are a series of addresses he has made to forces or conditions that have no ordinary means of receiving communication.

He has spoken to time. Directly to the phenomenon itself — to the quality of sequential existence, the river of before-and-after that carries everything and is itself carried by nothing. He has spoken to it in the manner of someone who has a legitimate grievance and intends to be heard, regardless of whether hearing is a capacity the interlocutor possesses.

He has spoken to evil. Not to the Devil in the theological sense — though that conversation, the one in which he called out into the darkness of a night projection and invited confrontation, is recorded elsewhere in this book — but to the principle of evil itself, the force of diminishment and corruption and the slow destruction of what is good, which he takes seriously as a real factor in the world rather than a metaphor for human weakness.

He has spoken to the ruins of the village that will be ruins in a hundred years. He stood in the future rubble and addressed the stones, the collapsed walls, the door hanging from its broken hinge, and said: I see you. I know what you were. I know what you will be. And I am telling you, though you cannot hear me, that the lives lived inside you were real, and that the love that filled you was real, and that time's capacity to erase the physical evidence of things does not erase the things themselves.

"The stone does not know it was once part of a wall that sheltered a family. But the family knows. And the knowing matters, even if the stone will never hear about it."

The most important of these impossible addresses was one he made in Marrakech, in the dark room, in the moment after the false memory of his father's presence had dissolved and the truth had returned with the force of cold water. He did not speak aloud. But in the specific register of interior address that is neither thought nor speech — the register in which genuine prayer occurs, whatever tradition one prays in — he said to his father:

"I am not finished with what you gave me. I am nowhere near finished. The books you brought from Fes have not stopped opening. The Quran you read to me on the rooftop has not stopped sounding. The instruction you gave me — hold to the religion of God, do not covet what belongs to others — I am working on it. Every day I am working on it. I do not always succeed. But I am working on it."

And then, because honesty requires this:

"I went to places you never went. I left the body and traveled in ways that the tradition does not endorse without serious qualification. I did things that were, by certain measures, inadvisable. I opened doors that took time to close. I am not certain I would do all of it differently, given who I was and what I needed to understand. But I closed the doors. I am standing on ground I believe is solid. And the instruction you gave me is the compass I use to check."

He does not know if his father heard this. He does not need to know. The address was genuine. The quality of interior attention it required — the honesty, the specificity, the refusal to perform a communication that was not actually occurring — was itself a form of practice. The practice of being fully present with what is actually happening, without performance, without escape into the comfortable approximation.

"Speak to your dead as you would speak to your living. They deserve the same honesty. And the discipline of speaking honestly to those who cannot contradict you is excellent preparation for speaking honestly to those who can."

He thinks his father would have found the out-of-body experiences interesting. Ibrahim was not a man frightened by the non-ordinary. He was a man who had encountered enough of it, in the long tradition of Moroccan rural spiritual life, to know that the world contains more than ordinary consciousness accesses. He would have asked questions. He would have offered the occasional dry observation. He would have advised, at some point, with the patient precision of someone who has been watching carefully, that whatever was being built should be built on the correct foundation. Which is, of course, exactly what Marudai eventually concluded on his own. The father's wisdom does not require the father's presence to continue operating. This is one of the things that wisdom means.
