The Father, The Rooftop, and The Stars

"My father was not literate in the way schools define literacy. But he read the human soul with an accuracy that no degree has ever conferred."

Ibrahim Bouhaj was a man who traveled to Fes and came back carrying books. This is the essential fact about him. Everything else — his weathered hands, his patient eyes, his voice that deepened when he read aloud, the way he could identify a man's true character in the time it takes to pour a glass of tea — flows from this central truth: he believed that knowledge was the most important thing a person could possess, and he transmitted this belief to his son not through instruction but through example.

In the evenings, in the season when the summer heat had eased enough to make the rooftop habitable after dark, Ibrahim would spread a mat on the concrete and lie on his back with his son beside him. The Milky Way was visible from that rooftop in a way it is not visible from cities — a smear of light across the black sky that looks, if you look at it long enough, less like stars and more like a river running through some country that has no name in any human language.

Ibrahim would recite. Whatever he had memorized from the Quran, whatever had lodged itself in his memory over decades of listening and repeating — the beautiful Surahs, the ones whose rhythms settle into the body like a second heartbeat — he would recite until the boy's breathing slowed and the stars blurred at the edges and sleep descended like a hand placed gently over the eyes.

"The stars do not care whether you know their names. They illuminate equally the scholar and the fool. But only the scholar knows what he is seeing. And only the mystic knows what the scholar has missed."

There was a peculiarity to these nights that Marudai would not understand until much later: he never actually fell asleep on the rooftop. He would pass through something — a threshold, a membrane, a soft boundary between waking and sleeping that most people cross without noticing — and emerge on the other side into a state that was neither sleep nor waking but something more coherent than either. He could see the stars more clearly in this state. He could hear his father's voice more precisely. And sometimes, without any effort or intention on his part, he could see other things as well.

Once, suspended in this state, he saw his father not as the familiar figure lying beside him but as something much older. A presence that had been present in various forms across a very long span of time — not reincarnation in the Hindu sense, not anything that his religious tradition would have endorsed as doctrine, but something that the tradition also acknowledged in its more esoteric registers: the persistence of the ruh, the spirit-substance, across configurations of matter. His father glowed faintly in this vision. A warm amber light, like a lamp behind thick glass. And his face, which was the face Marudai knew from every morning of his life, was also somehow the face of someone much older, much quieter, much more at peace with whatever came next.

He did not tell his father about this vision. But he held it. He held it the way you hold water in cupped hands — carefully, without squeezing, knowing that the moment you try to grip it is the moment it escapes.

The Inheritance of the Book

In 2008, Ibrahim brought home a copy of Riyad al-Salihin — Gardens of the Righteous — by Imam al-Nawawi. He had obtained it from a bookshop in old Fes, the kind of shop that smells of dust and leather and the accumulated intellectual labor of centuries, and he had carried it home on a six-hour bus journey as carefully as if it were made of something more fragile than paper.

The boy read it in a day and a half. Not skimming — reading, in the deep sense, which means allowing the words to change the shape of the mind that receives them. He finished more than half of it in the first sitting, stopping only when his eyes began to burn and his mother called him for the third time to come and eat.

Ibrahim listened as his son read to him from the book. He could follow Arabic with his ears when the text was clear and well-read — the language had entered him through the prayer tradition, through the Friday sermons, through decades of careful listening — and he offered responses that showed an understanding of the material that surprised even his son. He had his own way of knowing, Ibrahim did. It did not require literacy in the formal sense. It required only the quality of attention that some people are born with and others spend decades attempting to acquire.

"Do not covet the world. Hold to the religion of God. Do not desire what belongs to others — for the faithful, pious person is better than all others by virtue of their faith alone."

This was Ibrahim's instruction to his son, repeated with the patient insistence of someone who knows that some lessons must be heard many times before they are genuinely received. It sounds simple. It is not simple. It is, in fact, the most demanding instruction a human being can be given — because it asks for nothing less than the complete reorientation of the desire-structure that ordinary socialization installs. To stop coveting. To hold to what is true. To refuse the comparative logic that makes other people's having a measure of one's own lacking.

Marudai would spend years wrestling with this instruction. He would fail it in various directions before he found his way to something like genuine embodiment of it. But the seed was planted on those rooftop nights, under that river of light, by a man who had no formal education and absolute understanding.

Ibrahim passed from this world in a year that Marudai still does not name easily. The grief came not in the moment of the departure — it came later, in a dark room in Marrakech, many months afterward, when the subconscious mind finally surfaced with what it had been holding, and Marudai spent a full minute in absolute confusion, certain he was back in the village, certain his father was sleeping in the room across the hall, certain that the familiar weight of his father's presence had not yet lifted from the house. And then the truth returned, and it returned the way truths of that order always return — not gently, not with any preparation, but all at once, like cold water, like a stone wall, like the hard edge of a world that does not stop being real simply because we would prefer it to be otherwise.

He breathed. He endured. He kept the instruction.
