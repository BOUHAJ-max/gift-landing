First Departure — The Mountains of Nowhere

"The first time you leave the body, you do not go far. The second body is uncertain of itself, like a foal learning its legs. But it remembers. And it learns fast."

The early projections were modest in scope. They established a baseline of perception that all subsequent journeys would build upon — a register of experience that Marudai came to think of as the floor of the possible, from which everything else rose.

Each morning after the Fajr prayer, in those first months of late 2017, he would return to bed with the specific intention of allowing projection to occur if the conditions were right. He never forced it. The state arrived, when it arrived, with its characteristic electrical signature — a rising hum in the body, a particular quality of stillness that was not ordinary relaxation, a sense that the boundaries between himself and the air around him had become permeable. He learned to greet this state as one greets a frequent but unpredictable guest: with readiness, with respect, without urgency.

The first projection of this period transported him, without transition, to a mountain landscape he has never been able to identify with certainty on any physical map. The mountains were high and dark — their rock a deep charcoal color, almost black, veined with lighter minerals that caught what little light there was and held it. They were covered, in their lower reaches, by an extremely dense, deep green forest of a kind that grows only where moisture is generous and light is filtered. The sky above them was not visible in the ordinary sense; instead, a thick white fog filled the upper third of the visual field, settling into the valleys between peaks and obscuring the horizon with a completeness that felt absolute.

He did not move through this landscape. He was present in it — in the way that a complete sensory system is present in a space, with information arriving from every direction simultaneously, without the bottleneck of eyes-and-ears that physical experience imposes. The smell of the air was cold and piney and utterly real, with a complexity that no perfume laboratory has ever replicated: the base note of cold stone, the middle note of wet moss, the high note of something flowering in the darkness below the treeline. The sound of wind through the trees was complex and layered in a way that no recording has ever captured — each tree contributing a slightly different frequency, the ensemble creating a soundscape of remarkable depth.

"The body you leave behind is not the only body you have. It is the heaviest one. The other is lighter than thought and faster than light. It has been waiting for you to use it."

He remained in this place for what felt like five minutes of subjective time, simply attending. Then the familiar pull began — the physical body calling the consciousness home, an obligation that in those early months could not be refused — and he returned through the transition in reverse: a narrowing of the sensory field, a return of the electrical hum, a settling back into the weight and warmth of the physical form lying on the bed.

He lay still afterward for a long time. The quality of ordinary perception — the flat, two-dimensional ceiling above him, the familiar sounds of the house, the smell of his own room — seemed reduced by comparison. Not diminished in any way that caused distress. Simply: reduced. Like switching from a high-resolution photograph to a standard-definition image. Everything was there. The detail was simply less.

The House of His Grandfather

The second significant early projection took him to his grandfather's house, approximately fifty-eight kilometers from where his physical body lay. This distance he knew precisely because he had traveled it in waking life many times, in buses that took the mountain road slowly and stopped at villages along the way.

The arrival was instantaneous. One moment he was in the transitional phase — that particular electrical hum, the sense of the body becoming both very heavy and very light simultaneously; the next, he was standing in the guest room of his grandfather's house. The room was empty of people. Afternoon light fell across the tile floor in the specific pattern he knew from childhood visits, the light that came through the window that faced west and caught the late sun at an angle that made the old tiles glow amber.

He descended to floor level to examine the tiles. The cracks were exactly right. Their pattern was consistent with what he knew of that room — the long diagonal fracture near the window, the cluster of smaller cracks near the door where something heavy had once been dropped. Near one of the cracks, a small coin lay on the floor. He could see its color and approximate size but could not read any inscription from his angle, and he chose not to move closer, not because he could not but because something in the experience counseled stillness, observation, the discipline of not interfering.

He did not attempt to verify the coin's presence through waking means. This was an early projection; he was still learning the territory. But the precision of the room's physical details stayed with him as a marker of the projection's reality-quality — its claim to something more than internal imagination.
