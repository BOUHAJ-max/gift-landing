The Village That Will Be Ruins

"I have wept for people who are not yet dead. I have mourned a future that has not yet arrived. And I came back knowing that grief is not about time. It is about love without limit."

This is the experience that affected him most deeply. Not because of what he saw — though what he saw was striking, and strange, and in its own way beautiful with the beauty of honesty about how things end. But because of what he felt. Because of the grief.

It was summer. He had been sleeping on the rooftop of the family house in his village, as they did in the hot months when the inside of the house held the day's heat long after the sun had set and the sky had opened into stars. He woke before Fajr, prayed, and returned to the mat on the rooftop. The air was cool by then, carrying the particular mineral smell of the mountains at four in the morning — a smell that belongs only to that hour, in that place, and nowhere else in the world.

The projection came quickly, as it often did in the summer of that year. He had been practicing regularly. His technique had become efficient. He separated from the body cleanly and stood on the edge of the rooftop, looking out at the village.

He knew it well: the cluster of stone-and-mud houses, most of them old, their architecture the traditional Berber style of the region — thick walls, small windows, flat rooftops that served as a second floor in the evenings. The small mosque with its simple minaret, perhaps sixty meters away, the same mosque where he had learned to pray as a small boy, standing in the back row too short to see over the shoulders of the men in front of him.

"Time is not a river moving in one direction. Time is an ocean. You can stand in 2019 and look across to 2119, if you know how to look. What you see will not comfort you. But it will make you love the present with a ferocity you did not have before."

He had been thinking, in the days before this projection, about time. Specifically about the future — what would survive, what would change, what would be lost. One hundred years felt like a meaningful interval: long enough that significant change would be visible, short enough that the landscape itself would remain recognizable.

He decided to try it. Not with certainty that it was possible. With the experimental spirit that had driven all of his practice: try it, see what happens, report honestly.

The method he improvised was entirely intuitive. He gathered his attention into a focused point directed toward a space ten meters ahead of him — a specific place on the rooftop, a point in the ordinary space of ordinary time. He held in his intention the destination: one hundred years forward. And then, with the full force of concentrated will, he threw himself toward it.

Something happened.

The movement was not spatial. It was qualitative — a shift in the register of what was visible, a change in the character of the light and the texture of the surfaces and the feeling of the air. The village was still there around him. The sky was still above him. The same mountains framed the same horizon. But the village was different.

The Ruins

The mosque was partially collapsed. Its wall facing toward Mecca had lost perhaps a third of its height to decades of rain and wind and the slow retreat of maintenance. Through the gaps in the wall, he could see the interior — the ceiling partially fallen, wooden beams lying across the prayer area, their wood bleached and split by exposure to a sky that the ceiling had once kept out.

The houses were worse. Most of them had lost their roofs. Walls that had been intact were now reduced to waist height in places, or gone entirely. The village road was no longer identifiable as a road — water had cut new channels across it over decades of unmanaged flow, creating shallow ravines that had reorganized the terrain according to gravity's patient logic rather than the human desire for passage.

The large argan tree that had stood at the corner of the space where the donkeys were tethered — an old tree, a hundred years old at least even in the present time of the projection's origin — was gone. Its root system had been undermined by water erosion. What remained was a depression in the earth, and in the depression, the ghost of a root structure, and the memory of a tree.

His uncle's house — identifiable by its position even though most of its distinguishing features had been taken by time — had its door frame still standing. The door itself was broken at the base, rotated half-open on a hinge that could no longer bear its weight, held at an angle by the resistance of the earth that had accumulated in the doorway over years of disuse.

And then the grief arrived. Not as a thought. Not as a response to something seen. As a direct experience — immediate, physical, total. The grief of knowing that every person he loved, every voice he had ever heard in that village, every hand that had ever handed him a glass of tea in one of these houses — all of them were gone. Not soon. Already. One hundred years is enough time to take everyone.

He sat on a rock in the ruins and could not move. The grief pressed on the etheric body the way sorrow presses on the physical chest in waking life, but amplified beyond any range his ordinary emotional experience had mapped. He understood, in that moment, what the Sufi masters mean when they speak of the ahwal — the spiritual states — as experiences of a different order than ordinary emotion. This was not sadness at a manageable intensity. This was sadness at the intensity of the Real, unmediated by the psychological defenses that ordinary life keeps carefully in place.

The body recalled him before he was ready to go. He woke on the summer rooftop with the mountains still intact and the mosque still whole and the road still running through the village as it always had. But the grief stayed with him for hours. He lay on the mat and looked at the village in the early morning light and could not entirely separate what he was seeing from what he had seen — the intact present from the ruined future, both of them equally real, both of them equally his.
