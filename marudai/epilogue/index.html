Marudai Walks On

"The ships sail where the winds do not wish. And I am the wind. I am also the ship. I am also the sea. Choose which one you want to argue with."

He was nine years old the first time he noticed it. He was thirty-something the last time he mentioned it in a book. Between those two points, a man was made — not finished, never finished, but made in the sense that matters: made aware of his own nature, made familiar with the territory of the possible, made responsible for what he now knows.

Marudai walks on. The path is not straight. The path is not marked. The path is, to a considerable extent, the walking itself — the specific quality of attention brought to each step, each encounter, each experience of the ordinary and the extraordinary that life, in its inexhaustible creativity, continues to produce.

He carries, as a kind of internal compass, the instruction his father gave him on the rooftop under the stars: Hold to the religion of God. Do not covet what belongs to others. The faithful, pious person is better than all others by virtue of their faith alone.

He carries, as a kind of internal fire, the knowledge that the body is not the limit of the self — that consciousness is more portable, more resilient, and more capable than the ordinary world's insistence on ordinary categories would suggest.

He carries, as a kind of wound that has become a compass, the memory of the ruined village and the grief that came with it. Not as a warning against caring about things that will be lost. As a reminder that caring about things that will be lost is the only sane response to being alive in a world where everything will, eventually, be lost. Care completely. Grieve honestly. Continue.

"I am not the last rebel because I am the last. I am the last rebel because each genuine rebel is always the last — the one who refuses to hand the fire forward into safe hands, who insists on carrying it himself, all the way to the end."

He does not know what comes next. Neither does the yin. Neither does the yang. Neither does the Dragon, who breathes in the darkness between the symbols on a book cover, waiting to see what the reader will make of all this fire.

The question the book leaves you with is the same question it began with: What are you, beyond this body? And what would it cost you to find out?

Marudai walks on. The story has no last page. Only the book does.

— Mohamed Bouhaj, Casablanca, 2024
