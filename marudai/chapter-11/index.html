Forces of Light, Forces of Darkness

"Not all darkness is evil, and not all light is good. But some darkness is very specifically not your friend. Learn the difference before you invite it in."

The framework that made sense of everything came, as the most important intellectual discoveries tend to, not from the direction Marudai had been looking but from the periphery of his search — a series of books he had been told about by someone who had been told about them by someone else, published in Morocco in the decade before he was born, by scholars whose names he had encountered only as footnotes in other books.

Dr. Tihami Mohammed El-Wakili and Professor Nour-Eddine Abdelhaq produced, under the imprint of Dar al-A'maq — the House of the Depths — a series of volumes that addressed what they called the esoteric sciences of religion. This was not mysticism in the vague popular sense. It was systematic, analytical, and grounded in the classical Islamic sciences, while extending those sciences into territory that the orthodox tradition was reluctant to map. The volumes dealt with the subtle body, the science of movements, the inner world and its various registers, the dangers of misdirected practice, and the difference between genuine spiritual development and its many convincing counterfeits.

The central thesis of the movement science volume — Sirr al-Harakat — was, as we have seen, that every physical position the body adopts attracts a specific category of energy, classifiable as either forces of light or forces of darkness. But the implications of this thesis, as Marudai worked through them, extended far beyond sleeping positions.

"Every prayer posture is a conversation. Every prostration is a negotiation with a specific register of the Unseen. The question is not whether you are having this conversation. The question is whether you know what you are saying."

The prayer movements prescribed in Islam — the specific postures of qiyam (standing), ruku (bowing), sujud (prostration), julud (sitting) — were, in the framework of this tradition, not arbitrary physical forms chosen for their symbolism or their aesthetic fitness. They were precise energetic configurations, each one designed to attract a specific register of divine energy and direct it through the subtle body in a specific sequence, producing the specific developmental effect that the prayer — as a technology of consciousness as much as an act of worship — was designed to produce.

This was why the Prophet, peace be upon him, was so precise about the details of prayer. Why he specified the angle of the feet in prostration, the position of the hands in standing, the specific words to be said at specific moments in the prayer cycle. These were not arbitrary rules imposed from outside. They were instructions from someone who understood the mechanism.

They were, in a word, technology. The oldest and most advanced technology available to the human being: the technology of the self, operating on the self, to produce in the self the qualities that make genuine relationship with the Real possible.

The Green Force

Among the forces of darkness described in the Dar al-A'maq tradition, one category received particular attention: what the authors called the green force, al-quwwa al-khadra al-khabitha — the malicious green energy. This was described as the specific energy employed by the great sorcerers of history, including those of ancient Egypt, and as accessible through specific movement sequences that had been deliberately constructed or corrupted to function as invocations of this force.

Marudai treated this claim initially with careful skepticism. He had seen enough in his years of practice to know that forces of some kind were being attracted by different positions and practices — his left-side sleeping experiment had made that undeniable at the level of personal experience. But specific colors, specific ancient sorcerers: these felt, at first, like the language of folklore rather than the language of genuine esoteric science.

He revised this view after a series of experiences that he chooses not to describe in detail — not from evasion but from a genuine concern that description would mislead more than it would illuminate. What he will say is this: there are configurations of energy in the invisible world that are not neutral. There are forces that, once attracted, behave in ways that are recognizable and consistent — ways that the tradition has mapped with considerable precision, and that the practitioner ignores at genuine cost.

He closed the door. Deliberately and completely. He chose the path of the tradition over the path of the experiment. And he found, on the other side of that choice, a quality of peace that the experiment, for all its fascination, had never produced.
