The Coffee House of the Ecstatics

"The ecstatic does not choose his state. The state chooses him. And it cares nothing for the propriety of the occasion."

Marudai encountered the coffee house of the ecstatics not in a vision, not in projection, but in the space between — that register of experience that the tradition calls the barzakh, the intermediate realm, the zone where the imaginal world and the physical world share a single surface and the rules of neither apply completely.

The figure who received him was disheveled in the way that suggests long-term philosophical preoccupation rather than simple neglect: hair that had not been organized in weeks, a robe of indeterminate color — it might once have been grey, or it might always have been the color of smoke — and hands with an index finger swollen and weeping at the knuckle from some unspecified cause. He looked, at first assessment, like someone whose obvious virtue had been so thoroughly tested by experience that it had been refined to something purer and harder than virtue usually gets to be.

He looked at Marudai with the focused attention of someone reading a very complex text in a language they have spent years acquiring. He looked at his hands. He looked at the lines of his face. He looked at something behind his eyes that was not visible to ordinary seeing. And then he began to laugh — the particular hysterical laughter of someone who has been waiting for a specific absurdity and has finally had it confirmed.

"You think you are in a real world. You are in a lesson. The difference is that lessons end. What you take from them does not."

The other inhabitants of the coffee house — men in various stages of ecstatic remove, some muttering, some silent, some engaged in conversations with interlocutors that only they could perceive — reacted to Marudai's arrival with the collective awareness of a group of people who are individually removed from ordinary reality but collectively maintain a kind of distributed attention to what is actually happening in any given space. They began to laugh as well, and to mutter, and to make sounds that were recognizable as words in some configuration that fell just short of sentences Marudai could parse.

The main figure silenced them with a single breath — a controlled exhalation aimed in their direction that seemed to carry with it some property beyond air, some quality that could be described as authority without effort, or pressure without force. They subsided immediately, several of them folding forward as though briefly unconscious.

"Do not concern yourself with them. They are the weak-minded. I will deal with them in their appropriate time."

He took a single long draw from a cigarette — hand-rolled, the tobacco dark and strong — and blew the smoke directly into Marudai's face. The smoke was not ordinary smoke. It was hot in a way that had nothing to do with combustion, a heat that came from the direction of depth rather than the direction of temperature, and it set something vibrating in his chest that continued to vibrate for a long time afterward.

"What you think is reality is not reality. It is a lesson prepared for you. You will understand its meaning when God has decided the time for you to understand it. You will disappear from here now. You deserve to live a better life than ours, which is the miserable life of those trapped in the world of imagination. There is no place for you among us, and no place for us among you."

He pressed his thumb to Marudai's forehead and whispered a single word that Marudai heard not with his ears but with some faculty further interior than hearing.

He woke under an olive tree near the family house, to the sound of the midday call to prayer, with no memory of lying down.
