The Philosophy of the Rebel

"Rebellion is not the opposite of order. It is the demand for a deeper order — one that has not yet been built, one that the current arrangement cannot accommodate, one that will require the destruction of what is insufficient before the construction of what is necessary."

The word rebel carries, in contemporary usage, the smell of adolescence — the teenager slamming the door, the young artist rejecting their parents' values, the political dissident writing manifestos in garrets. It has been domesticated into a brand, stamped onto T-shirts, turned into a posture that sells things. Marudai wants to reclaim it from all of this and return it to something closer to its origin.

The genuine rebel — the one he means when he applies the word to himself, the one he means when he names his protagonist Marudai, which means the rebel in the Syriac tongue of ancient mystics — is not primarily in rebellion against other people. He is in rebellion against the insufficiency of the available. Not the insufficiency of the available material world — though that too, in the sense that the material world's claim to be the only world is a claim the evidence does not support. The insufficiency of the available explanations. The insufficiency of the available frameworks. The insufficiency of the available responses to what is actually the case.

When Marudai calls himself a rebel, he means this: he has refused to accept that the world as it presents itself to ordinary consciousness is the whole of what is there. He has refused to accept that the body is the limit of the self. He has refused to accept that consciousness is a byproduct of matter rather than the other way around — or rather, he has refused to accept the certainty in either direction, insisting instead on the epistemological honesty that the question is still genuinely open. He has refused to accept the comfortable versions of religion that soothe without transforming, the comfortable versions of science that explain without illuminating, the comfortable versions of mysticism that decorate the surface of ordinary life without touching its foundation.

"The rebel does not know what the right answer is. The rebel knows that the available answers are not sufficient. This is different from nihilism. Nihilism says there is no right answer. Rebellion says: there is one, and we have not found it yet, and the not-finding is not a reason to stop looking."

Jibran Khalil Jibran — whom Marudai acknowledges as a predecessor, though he insists on certain differences — was in rebellion against the structures of his society: the church, the class system, the conventions that diminished life by making it safe. His rebellion was fundamentally social. Its energy was directed outward, toward the institutions and arrangements of the human world.

Marudai's rebellion is directed at a deeper level. Not at the social arrangements — though those have their share of insufficiency and he has no particular affection for pretension in any of its forms — but at the metaphysical arrangements: the agreements about what is real, what is possible, what human beings are and what they can become. These agreements are encoded not just in institutions but in the structure of ordinary consciousness itself, in the habits of perception and interpretation that constitute what we call reality.

He has spent years testing these agreements against his direct experience and finding them wanting. Not false — wrong is too strong a word for arrangements that work reasonably well for most purposes most of the time. Insufficient. Unable to account for what he has actually experienced, witnessed, and verified. Unable to provide an adequate framework for the range of human possibility that the tradition he studies claims is available.

So he rebels. Not dramatically. Not with manifestos and gestures. With the quiet, persistent, completely unglamorous rebellion of someone who refuses to pretend that the available explanations are adequate when they are not. Who refuses to call the experiences he has had hallucinations when hallucination does not explain the flood that was verified an hour later. Who refuses to call the inner world imaginary when the imaginary does not generate the specific quality of information that the inner world sometimes generates.

The Dragon Is a Stance

The dragon on the cover of this book — purple, because purple is the color of the boundary between the earthly and the divine, between blood and sky, between what is and what could be — is not a monster. It is not a symbol of evil or chaos or destruction, though it can produce all three when pointed in the wrong direction.

The dragon is a stance. A specific quality of engagement with existence that refuses both the comfort of easy answers and the despair of no answers. That maintains, in the face of everything that argues for simplification and settlement, the insistence on the full complexity of what is actually there.

The dragon breathes fire not at random and not at everything. It breathes fire at the false — the pretended, the performed, the comfortable lie told in the name of peace or order or social functioning. It breathes fire at the version of religion that is really conformity dressed in theological language. At the version of spiritual development that is really status competition in a different register. At the version of scientific materialism that is really the refusal to follow the evidence wherever it leads out of loyalty to a paradigm that has become more important than the truth it was designed to discover.

This is the rebellion Marudai embodies. Not against God. Never against God. Against the distance between what is actually possible for a human being and what most human beings are prepared to demand of themselves. Against the conspiracy of mediocrity that presents itself as humility. Against the version of acceptance that is really surrender to a reality that is far stranger and far richer and far more demanding than the settled agreements allow.

"Nietzsche said God is dead and we have killed him. He was wrong about the first part. He was right about the second. What dies, in the history of every civilization that loses its connection to the genuinely sacred, is not God. It is the human being's capacity to perceive God. And yes, we killed it. And yes, we can revive it. This is the work."

He does not claim to have completed this work. He claims to have begun it, to be continuing it, to be somewhere on the path between where he started and where the tradition says it is possible to arrive. The beginning was a nine-year-old boy in a schoolyard feeling the hairs on his arms rise before a wall collapsed. The continuation is a man in his thirties praying five times daily and reading and writing and refusing, each day, to settle for the available version of what is possible.

The end, if there is an end, is not in this book. It is not in any book. It is in the living of a life that takes seriously what the tradition says the life can become — not in theory, not as metaphor, but in the actual, daily, embodied practice of becoming what you are.

Marudai walks on. The dragon breathes. The yin and the yang continue their eternal negotiation. The work continues.
