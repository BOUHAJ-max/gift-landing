What I Would Tell the Beginner

"The most useful thing an experienced traveler can tell the beginner is not where to go. It is how to recognize when you are lost — and what to do when you are."

This chapter is addressed directly to the person who has picked up this book because they are experiencing things they cannot explain. The person who has woken in the night with their body paralyzed and the room full of presences. The person who has slipped, without intention, out of the physical body and found themselves floating in a familiar room, looking at a familiar sleeping form and knowing with certainty that the sleeping form is theirs. The person who has had, in meditation or prayer or simply in the quiet of an early morning, an experience of such perceptual expansion and emotional intensity that the ordinary world has seemed, ever since, slightly smaller than it did before.

If this is you: you are not ill. You are not losing your mind. You are not being deceived by your own overactive imagination, though imagination is certainly involved. You have encountered the non-ordinary, which has the inconvenient quality of being real even when it would be more comfortable to dismiss it. Here is what experience — mine and the tradition's — suggests about how to proceed.

First: Get the Foundation Right

Before you explore the non-ordinary with any seriousness, make sure your ordinary life is as solid as you can make it. Not perfect — no one's ordinary life is perfect. But grounded. The non-ordinary amplifies everything: courage becomes recklessness; sensitivity becomes inability to function; the desire to understand becomes the construction of elaborate systems that have nothing in common with genuine knowledge. Without a solid ordinary foundation — a real relationship with the world, with other people, with honest work, with the simple dailiness of a life being genuinely lived — the non-ordinary will unbalance you.

Second: Get a Map

Do not explore without a map. Not because the map is always right — it is not. But because having a map gives you something to check your experience against, something to use as a reference when the territory becomes disorienting, something to return to when you have gone further than the map covers and need to find your way back. The Islamic esoteric tradition, as I have encountered it through the Dar al-A'maq framework, is the most useful map I have found. For those who are not operating within the Islamic tradition, find the most rigorous and most grounded map available within your own tradition, and use it.

Third: Do Not Seek the Extraordinary

This sounds counterintuitive in a book that has spent hundreds of pages describing extraordinary experiences. But the extraordinary, when it comes, will come whether or not you are seeking it. The seeking itself tends to produce the imaginal rather than the real, the self-generated rather than the genuinely encountered, the dramatic rather than the true. The experiences I trust most in my own record are the ones that arrived without invitation, that surprised me, that did not confirm what I expected and did not produce what I wanted.

Fourth: Verify What You Can

When an experience gives you information that can be checked — check it. The flood. The floor of my grandfather's house. Note these things and verify them when verification is possible. Note also when verification is not possible — the world with the red hills, the rings at the edge of the universe — and hold those experiences with appropriate epistemic humility. The practice of verification does not diminish the value of the experience. It calibrates it. It helps you distinguish between genuine kashf (unveiling) and khiyal (imaginal self-generation). Both are real. They are not equally reliable as sources of knowledge.

"The honest explorer marks on the map: here is where I went, here is what I found, here is what I verified, here is what I could not verify, here is where the map ran out. This is not weakness. This is the only method that produces genuine knowledge."

One final thing, and it is not practical instruction but a statement of what he actually believes after everything: the world you were born into is not the limit of what exists. The body you inhabit is not the limit of what you are. The ordinary consciousness that accompanies you through most of your waking hours is not the only mode of awareness available to you.

But the non-ordinary world is not safer than the ordinary world, and expanded consciousness is not automatically wiser than ordinary consciousness. It is larger. It is not, by virtue of its largeness, automatically more oriented toward what is genuinely true and genuinely good. That orientation requires work — the specific, daily, undramatic, ordinary work of a life that is trying to become what the tradition says it is possible for a human life to become.

Begin there. Begin with the ordinary, faithfully attended to, with the full quality of consciousness you can bring to it. The extraordinary will find you when it is ready. It always does.
