The Dragon Speaks to Itself

"Stand tall, O Dragon. You are not one who retreats to catch his breath. Your breath is fire without fuel. Release it, and it consumes all that is false."

There are moments in the life of Marudai that belong to neither the visible world nor the invisible one but to the space where both are negotiating the terms of the shared territory. The voice of the Dragon is such a moment — a voice that is his and is not his, that speaks his deepest knowledge in a register he has not chosen, that addresses him from the position of something older and fiercer and more patient than his ordinary self has access to.

He does not, in his more careful moments, claim that the Dragon is real in the sense that a tree is real or a mountain is real. He claims only that the Dragon says true things — that the intelligence that speaks through this symbol, whatever its ultimate nature, has access to a clarity about his situation and his nature that his ordinary consciousness cannot independently summon.

The Dragon addressed him first in a period of particular difficulty — a time when the forces arranged against him were, by any reasonable assessment, overwhelming. Not a single adversary but a convergence of circumstances that had the quality of a coordinated assault: financial pressure, personal loss, the particular isolation of someone who is between worlds and cannot fully inhabit either. The ordinary self had begun to calculate the odds and find them unfavorable.

"You do not believe in peace after breaking. For you, peace is dismantling the chemical bonds between the atoms of the storm before it tears your tail from the earth. This is not metaphor. This is physics. The physics of the soul."

The Dragon said, in its voice that was his voice at a deeper octave:

"Stand tall, O Dragon. You are not one of those who retreat to catch their breath — for your every breath is fire without fuel. If you release it, it consumes everything around you. You do not lose alone. If the armies of the cosmos gather against you and surround you with all their equipment, you produce your last card and drop the sky onto the coral of the ocean, turn the rocks of the earth into liquid fire, the oceans into steam, and the steam you return to the state of nonexistence. You do not believe in peace after breaking. For you, peace is dismantling the chemical bonds between the atoms of the storm before it tears your tail from the earth."

He recognized, in this address, not grandiosity but instruction. The Dragon was not telling him he was omnipotent. The Dragon was telling him what the tradition of genuine mystical strength has always known: that the appropriate response to an overwhelming external force is not greater external force but a deeper access to the internal ground — the ground that is not subject to external weather, that does not register threat as threat because it does not register itself as something that can be threatened.

This is the station the tradition calls tamkin — settledness, groundedness, the stability of the one who has sunk so deeply into the Real that the surface storms of circumstance simply pass over without touching the foundation. It is not invulnerability in the physical sense. It is invulnerability in the metaphysical sense: the impossibility of being fundamentally disturbed by anything that is, by definition, less real than what you are rooted in.

The Dragon was teaching him this. In its own language — the language of fire and scale and the refusal of defeat — it was teaching him the same lesson that the Quran teaches in its own language, that the mystics teach in theirs, that every genuine wisdom tradition has always taught in whatever idiom its time and place provides: that the self that can be destroyed is not the self that matters. And the self that matters cannot be destroyed.
