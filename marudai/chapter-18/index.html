On Human Beings and the Habit of Smallness

"How strange are the souls of some people. They speak as though they lit the sun themselves. Meanwhile their own souls drown in the mud of their vanities, unaware that the drowning is visible to everyone except themselves."

Marudai has spent enough time in the company of people who speak about spiritual development to have developed a reliable taxonomy of the ways in which the genuine impulse toward the transcendent gets hijacked by the ordinary human hunger for status, certainty, and the intoxicating sensation of being one of the few who really understand.

He has met the man who wears the green turban so brilliantly shined that the people around him cannot see anything except its reflection. The man has genuine gifts — Marudai does not dismiss this. He can perceive things. He has experiences that exceed ordinary psychological generation. But he has built around these gifts a structure of self-presentation so elaborate, so devoted to the production of a specific impression in specific audiences, that the gifts themselves have become secondary to the performance. He has confused the instrument for the music.

He has met the woman whose accurate intuitions — and they are real — have become a business model, a system, a franchise. She can tell you what the universe wants for you, for a fee. She can confirm that you are special, that your suffering is the prelude to a magnificent destiny, for a fee. Her clients love her. They come back. They bring their friends.

"The counterfeit is always more appealing than the genuine. The genuine requires you to change. The counterfeit lets you stay exactly as you are while feeling as though you have changed. This is why the market for counterfeits is inexhaustible."

He is not morally superior to these people. The same impulses that produce these outcomes — the desire to be recognized, the hunger for certainty, the comfort of having a defined role in the spiritual order — are present in him as well. He has acted on them. He has been wrong in ways that, in retrospect, are embarrassingly transparent. The difference — if there is one — is only in the response to the error. He has tried, when the error became visible, to acknowledge it as error rather than reframe it as a misunderstood step in the right direction.

Among the errors available to the spiritually developing person, the one he considers most dangerous is what he calls the error of the spiritual narcissist. The spiritual narcissist is not a fraud — this is what makes the error so dangerous. He has had genuine experiences. She has developed genuine sensitivity. But the experience, rather than dissolving the boundary between self and world, has been used to inflate the self: to make the self into the center of a universe now seen as revolving around its spiritual development, its journey, its destiny. This is the inversion of what genuine spiritual development produces.

The genuine encounter with the Real does not make you more important. It makes you more aware of how little importance has to do with anything that actually matters. The genuine encounter with the Real does not confirm your specialness. It reveals the absurdity of the category. Everything is special. Nothing is special. The universe does not organize itself around any individual's development, no matter how genuinely remarkable that development is.

"The one who emerges from genuine encounter with the Real does not say: now I know more than others. The one who emerges from genuine encounter with the Real says: I know how little I know. And this is not false modesty. It is the most accurate description available of what the encounter produces."

The antidote he has found — partial, imperfect, requiring constant re-administration — is the instruction his father gave him on the rooftop. Do not covet what belongs to others. The faithful pious person is better than all others by virtue of their faith alone. Not by virtue of their experiences. Not by virtue of their capacity to travel out of the body or witness events at a distance or visit the future ruins of villages. By virtue of their faith — which is their genuine orientation toward the Real, their genuine commitment to what is actually true and good, their genuine willingness to be changed by what they encounter rather than to use what they encounter to maintain an unchanged self in a more prestigious position.
